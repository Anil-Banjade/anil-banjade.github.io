Perspective-n-Point (PnP)

Perspective-n-Point (PnP) determines the 6-DoF absolute pose of a camera (extrinsic parameters \({R,t }\)), given a set of 3D–2D correspondences. A minimum of 3 points is required to solve for the pose. However, 3 points give four algebraically valid solutions, so a 4th point is required to disambiguate the correct one, and also using more points than 3 points also improves robustness against noise. In mapping systems like SLAM, PnP exploits an existing 3D map to estimate the camera pose when a new frame arrives.

1 Introduction

In the previous blog on epipolar geometry, we recovered the relative pose \((\mat{R}, \vect{t})\) between two views from 2D–2D correspondences using the epipolar constraint, and then triangulated those points to obtain 3D scene coordinates. However, epipolar geometry only gives a pose relative to the previous frame. If a third frame \(f_3\) arrives and we apply epipolar geometry between frames \(f_2\) and \(f_3\), we recover a relative pose that is not aligned with the world frame established by \(f_1\) and \(f_2\). Building a chain of these relative poses frame-to-frame accumulates drift, leading to a globally inconsistent map.

The Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem solves this by estimating the absolute pose of a new frame directly against the existing 3D map, utilizing 2D–3D correspondences. Once a 3D point \(\vect{X}_w\) triangulated from frames \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) is observed in frame \(f_3\), PnP recovers the absolute pose of \(f_3\) relative to the world frame, anchoring the trajectory to a consistent reference frame instead of drifting over time.

Let \(\vect{X}_w = (X_w, Y_w, Z_w)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3\) denote a 3D point expressed in the world coordinate frame, and let \(\vect{x} = (u, v, 1)^\top\) be the homogeneous pixel coordinate of its projection in the new frame. As previously mentioned in epipolar geometry blog, the normalized image coordinate is given by:

\[ \hat{\vect{x}} = \mat{K}^{-1}\vect{x} \]

where \(\mat{K}\) is the camera's intrinsic matrix, \(\hat{\vect{x}}\) is the direction of the ray from the optical center to the 3D point expressed in the camera frame, independent of depth.

Since \(\vect{X}_c/Z_c = \hat{\vect{x}}\), the 3D point in the camera frame lies along this ray at some positive depth \(\lambda = Z_c\) \(\tag{check Section 2.1 of the epipolar post for proof}\)

\[ \vect{X}_c = \lambda\, \hat{\vect{x}}, \qquad \lambda \in \mathbb{R}^+ \]

The rigid-body transformation from the world frame to the camera frame is given by the extrinsics \(\mat{R} \in SO(3)\), \(\vect{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3\):

\[ \vect{X}_c = \mat{R}\, \vect{X}_w + \vect{t} \]

Equating the ray and extrinsic equations gives the fundamental PnP constraint:

PnP constraint \[ \lambda\, \hat{\vect{x}} = \mat{R}\, \vect{X}_w + \vect{t} \]

2 The P3P Constraint

Rigid-body transformations preserve Euclidean distances, so the distance between any two 3D points is the same whether measured in the world frame or the camera frame. For three points \(\vect{X}_{w,1}, \vect{X}_{w,2}, \vect{X}_{w,3}\), define the side lengths of the triangle they form:

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= \|\vect{X}_{w,2} - \vect{X}_{w,1}\| = \|\vect{X}_{c,2} - \vect{X}_{c,1}\| \\ b &= \|\vect{X}_{w,3} - \vect{X}_{w,2}\| = \|\vect{X}_{c,3} - \vect{X}_{c,2}\| \\ c &= \|\vect{X}_{w,1} - \vect{X}_{w,3}\| = \|\vect{X}_{c,1} - \vect{X}_{c,3}\| \end{aligned} \]

and let \(s_i = \|\vect{X}_{c,i}\| = \lambda_i \|\hat{\vect{x}}_i\|\) be the unknown distance from the camera center to point \(i\). Applying the Law of Cosines to the three triangles formed by the camera center \(O\) and each pair of points,

\[ \begin{aligned} a^2 &= s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2 s_1 s_2 \cos \alpha, \\ b^2 &= s_2^2 + s_3^2 - 2 s_2 s_3 \cos \beta, \\ c^2 &= s_1^2 + s_3^2 - 2 s_1 s_3 \cos \gamma, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) are the angles subtended at the camera center as shown in the figure. Because these angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \) depend only on ray directions, they are computed directly from the image coordinates, independent of the unknown depths:

\[ \cos\alpha = \frac{\hat{\vect{x}}_1^\top \hat{\vect{x}}_2}{\|\hat{\vect{x}}_1\|\, \|\hat{\vect{x}}_2\|} \]

Similarly, angles \(\beta, \gamma\) can be calculated.

The side lengths \(a, b, c\) are known (they are distances between the triangulated 3D map points \(\vect{X}_{w,i}\)), and \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) are known from above. Therefore, above three equations have three unknown distances \(s_1, s_2, s_3\) which is the classical Perspective-Three-Point (P3P) problem.

A camera pose \((\mat{R}, \vect{t})\) has 6 degrees of freedom: 3 for rotation and 3 for translation. Because each 2D–3D point correspondence provides 2 constraints (\(u, v\)), a minimum of 3 points yields 6 equations to solve for the 6 unknowns. However, since the P3P equations are non-linear, this minimal system produces up to four valid solutions, requiring a 4th point to find the true pose.

2.1  Grunert's Solution

Instead of directly solving, we define ratios as following to simplify the calculation.
\[ u = \frac{s_2}{s_1}, \qquad v = \frac{s_3}{s_1} \]

Dividing the Law-of-Cosines equations by \(s_1^2\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \left(\frac{a}{s_1}\right)^2 &= 1 + u^2 - 2u \cos\alpha, \\ \Rightarrow s_1^2 &= \frac{a^2}{1+u^2-2u\cos\alpha} \qquad (1) \\ \left(\frac{b}{s_1}\right)^2 &= u^2 + v^2 - 2uv \cos\beta, \\ \Rightarrow s_1^2 &= \frac{b^2}{u^2+v^2-2uv\cos\beta} \qquad (2) \\ \left(\frac{c}{s_1}\right)^2 &= 1 + v^2 - 2v \cos\gamma, \\ \Rightarrow s_1^2 &= \frac{c^2}{1+v^2-2v\cos\gamma} \qquad (3) \end{aligned} \]

Combining (1) and (3):

\[ \frac{a^2}{c^2}(1+v^2-2v\cos\gamma) = 1+u^2-2u\cos\alpha \]

or,

\[ u^2 - \frac{a^2}{c^2}v^2 + \frac{2a^2}{c^2}v\cos\gamma - 2u\cos\alpha + \frac{c^2-a^2}{c^2} = 0 \qquad (4) \]

Combining (2) and (3):

\[ \frac{b^2}{c^2}(1+v^2-2v\cos\gamma) = u^2+v^2-2uv\cos\beta \]

or,

\[ u^2 + \left(\frac{c^2-b^2}{c^2}\right)v^2 - 2uv\cos\beta + \frac{2b^2}{c^2}u\cos\gamma - \frac{b^2}{c^2} = 0 \qquad (5) \]

or,

\[ u^2 = \frac{-c^2+b^2}{c^2}v^2 + 2uv\cos\beta - \frac{2b^2}{c^2}v\cos\gamma + \frac{b^2}{c^2} \qquad (6) \]

Putting (6) in (4), we get:

\[ \left(\frac{-c^2+b^2}{c^2}\right)v^2 + 2uv\cos\beta - \frac{2b^2}{c^2}v\cos\gamma + \frac{b^2}{c^2} - \frac{a^2}{c^2}v^2 + \frac{2va^2}{c^2}\cos\gamma - 2u\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2}{c^2} = 0 \]

or,

\[ v^2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2-c^2}{c^2}\right) + u(2v\cos\beta-2\cos\gamma) - 2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2}{c^2}\right)v\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2+b^2}{c^2} = 0 \]

Solving for \(u\):

\[ u = -\frac{\frac{b^2-a^2-c^2}{c^2}v^2 - 2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2}{c^2}\right)v\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2+b^2}{c^2}}{2v\cos\beta-2\cos\gamma} \]
\[ u = \frac{\frac{b^2-a^2-c^2}{c^2}v^2 - 2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2}{c^2}\right)v\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2+b^2}{c^2}}{2\cos\gamma-2v\cos\beta} \]

Putting \(u\) into (5), we get:

\[ \left(\frac{\frac{b^2-a^2-c^2}{c^2}v^2 - 2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2}{c^2}\right)v\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2+b^2}{c^2}}{2\cos\gamma-2v\cos\beta}\right)^2 + \frac{c^2-b^2}{c^2}v^2 - 2\left(\frac{\frac{b^2-a^2-c^2}{c^2}v^2 - 2\left(\frac{b^2-a^2}{c^2}\right)v\cos\gamma + \frac{c^2-a^2+b^2}{c^2}}{2\cos\gamma-2v\cos\beta}\right)v\cos\beta + \frac{2b^2}{c^2}v\cos\gamma - \frac{b^2}{c^2} = 0 \qquad (7) \]

This equation (7) is the fourth degree polynomial which can be expressed as

\[ A_4 v^4 + A_3 v^3 + A_2 v^2 + A_1 v + A_0 = 0 \]

This equation (7) have up to four real, positive roots for \(v\), and each root gives a corresponding value \(u\) which we can use to get values of \(s_1, s_2, s_3\).

Then we can recover the 3D point positions in the camera frame along each known ray direction as:

\[ \vect{X}_{c,i} = s_i\, \frac{\hat{\vect{x}}_i}{\|\hat{\vect{x}}_i\|}, \qquad i = 1,2,3. \]

3  Estimating the Pose \((\mat{R}, \vect{t})\)

Once \(\vect{X}_{c,1}, \vect{X}_{c,2}, \vect{X}_{c,3}\) are known, we have two matched point sets \(\{\vect{X}_{w,i}\}\) in the world frame and \(\{\vect{X}_{c,i}\}\) in the camera frame which are related by unknown rigid transform \(R, t\) for every candidate solution.

The centroids can be calculated as:

\[ \bar{\vect{X}}_w = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^3 \vect{X}_{w,i}, \qquad \bar{\vect{X}}_c = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^3 \vect{X}_{c,i}, \]

and the cross-covariance matrix

\[ \mat{H} = \sum_{i=1}^3 (\vect{X}_{w,i} - \bar{\vect{X}}_w)(\vect{X}_{c,i} - \bar{\vect{X}}_c)^\top \]

Taking the SVD \(\mat{H} = \mat{U}\, \mat{\Sigma}\, \mat{V}^\top\), the rotation and translation are

Pose from SVD \[ \mat{R} = \mat{V}\mat{U}^\top, \qquad \vect{t} = \bar{\vect{X}}_c - \mat{R}\, \bar{\vect{X}}_w. \]

If \(\det(\mat{R}) = -1\), the solution has produced a reflection which can be corrected as in previous epipolar post, by flipping the sign of the last column of \(\mat{V}\) and recomputing \(\mat{R}\).

4Resolving the Ambiguity

Each of the real positive roots from equation (7) produces a geometrically valid triangle configuration with the same side lengths \(a, b, c\) and the same subtended angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\). This is illustrated in the figure below from Cyrill Stachniss, though his notation is different from ours.

To select the correct one, a fourth point correspondence \((\vect{X}_{w,4}, \hat{\vect{x}}_4)\) is introduced. For each candidate pose, \(\vect{X}_{w,4}\) is transformed into the camera frame and reprojected:

\[ \hat{\vect{x}}_4^{\,\text{pred}} = \frac{\mat{R}\vect{X}_{w,4} + \vect{t}}{\big[\mat{R}\vect{X}_{w,4} + \vect{t}\big]_z}, \]

and the candidate whose predicted projection has the lowest reprojection error against the observed \(\hat{\vect{x}}_4\) is retained as the correct pose.

Note

In practice, with noisy correspondences and \(n > 4\) points, RANSAC algorithm is used which keeps the one with the most inliers, rather than relying on a single 4th point.
